CH · 01 The Candidates

Eyebrow — The Candidates

14 Years Creates a Record.

Barbara Kahl

Veterinarian • Former Intel Employee • Fourth-Generation Oregonian

Barbara Kahl is a fourth-generation Oregonian who graduated from Hillsboro High School and earned degrees in animal science and chemistry before becoming a veterinarian.[1]

She has spent nearly 20 years operating a mobile veterinary practice across the district and previously worked at Intel.[1]

In 2022, Kahl was recruited to serve as chief veterinarian for American Samoa, where she helped establish the territory’s first animal-control unit and drafted its Veterinary Practice Act.[1]

Her stated priorities are:

  • Economic growth
  • Timber and ports
  • Less regulatory burden
  • School choice
  • Education funding that follows the student
  • Lower healthcare costs
  • Federal accountability
  • Term limits[1]

Suzanne Bonamici

Member of Congress Since 2012

Suzanne Bonamici entered Congress through a 2012 special election and serves on the House Education and Workforce Committee and House Science, Space and Technology Committee.

Her recent political and congressional record includes:

  • Calls for less ICE funding[3]
  • Greater federal education funding[3]
  • Nay votes on the Laken Riley Act in 2024 and 2025
  • A Nay vote on H.Con.Res.9, a 2023 resolution “denouncing the horrors of socialism”
  • Climate-focused policy[3]
  • Immigration legislation and opposition to additional ICE detention capacity in Oregon[3][5]
  • Water-power and energy research legislation[4]
  • 2026 Oregon Working Families Party endorsement/cross-nomination[2]

The question is not whether she has experience.
It’s what that record shows after fourteen years.

CH · 02Economy

CD-1 makes things.

Semiconductors in Washington County. Freight through the Columbia. Timber from Northwest Oregon. Tourism and fishing on the coast. Small businesses throughout the district.

Federal decisions on permitting, energy, land use, workforce policy, and regulation can affect the businesses operating here.

Barbara Kahl

Barbara Kahl’s economic argument starts with production.

Her stated priorities include:

  • Timber supply.
  • Strong Columbia and coastal ports.
  • Less red tape on small businesses.
  • Workforce investment connected to real jobs.
  • Lower regulatory costs for builders and employers.[1]

Her experience includes both Intel and navigating federal and state veterinary regulation.[1]

Make it easier to build, grow, ship and hire.

Suzanne Bonamici

Bonamici’s economic agenda emphasizes STEAM education, expanded federal government investment, climate-related infrastructure and other federal programs.[3]

She has spent fourteen years representing a district containing one of Oregon’s most important manufacturing and technology corridors.[2]

After 14 years, her record is everywhere to analyse where she stands.

CH · 03Energy

CD-1 needs power.

Who pays for the next megawatt?

Homes need electricity. Intel and other manufacturers need reliable electricity. And rapidly expanding data centers and AI infrastructure can create significant additional electricity demand.

In January 2026, Bonamici introduced H.R. 7129, the Water Power Research and Development Reauthorization Act, with Rep. Nick Begich.[4]

The legislation addresses hydropower, marine energy and pumped-storage research, including integration with large electrical loads such as data centers.[4]

Bonamici’s 2025 voting record also includes Nay votes on:

  • H.R. 3632 — Power Plant Reliability
  • H.R. 3628 — State Planning for Reliability and Affordability
  • H.R. 1949 — Unlocking Domestic LNG
  • H.R. 1047 — GRID Power
  • H.R. 3015 — National Coal Council[5]

Barbara Kahl

Kahl argues that large-scale data-center demand should not leave households and manufacturers carrying disproportionate grid costs.

She has called for greater scrutiny of where regional energy is routed and how large-load growth affects existing users.[1]

Her point is “audit first”:

  • Where is the power going?
  • Who is creating the new demand?
  • Who is paying for the infrastructure?

The energy record clearly needs a closer look.

CH · 05Schools

Fund the student or fund the system?

Oregon’s education outcomes remain a major concern.

Barbara Kahl

Kahl supports:

  • Phonics
  • Teacher freedom
  • Vocational pathways
  • School choice
  • Funding that follows the student[1]

Her education argument is focused on student outcomes and giving families more control over where education dollars go.

Suzanne Bonamici

Bonamici serves on the House Education and Workforce Committee and is Ranking Member of its Early Childhood, Elementary, and Secondary Education Subcommittee.

She advocates greater federal government education spending and federal involvement in education policy.

Her campaign has also paired education funding with immigration-enforcement spending:

“Schools need more funding. ICE needs less.”

Bonamici has made education a major focus of her congressional work. Voters can compare that record with Oregon’s current education outcomes.

The policy questions

  • How much responsibility should Washington have for education?
  • Should additional federal spending be the priority, or should funding follow students and families?
  • What outcomes should accompany additional government spending?

CH · 06Healthcare

Healthcare: who deals with the price?

Barbara Kahl is a veterinarian and has spent nearly two decades operating a clinical practice.[1] Her healthcare argument starts with a basic problem:

Expanding coverage does not automatically make the underlying care less expensive.

Barbara Kahl

Kahl emphasizes:

  • Hospital costs
  • Drug prices
  • Keeping independent practices viable
  • Examining costs before creating new taxes
  • Scrutinizing the financing behind government healthcare proposals[1]

Fix the price before moving the bill.

Suzanne Bonamici

Bonamici frames healthcare as a moral and access issue and supports a larger public role in healthcare coverage.[3]

Her campaign has argued against increasing healthcare costs to finance tax reductions for wealthy Americans.[3]

Coverage and cost are not the same question.

CD-1 voters can examine which problem each candidate proposes to address first.
